What to capture for the "Build" Log, and what each deck slide should say

AThe "Build" Log — exactly what to submit

The brief asks for "screenshots of code, GitHub commit history, or version iterations." The strongest version of that is evidence a judge can verify without you in the room. These nine artifacts are all reproducible from the repository today; the commands are given.

#ArtifactHow to capture itWhy a judge cares
1Commit graph
highest value		 GitHub → Insights → Network, or the commits list showing all 12 commits and 5 merged PRs with dates. Shows a sustained build across four phases, not one weekend. The PR-per-phase structure reads as discipline.
2Green test run
strongest single image		 cd backend && .venv/bin/python -m pytest -v — capture the tail showing 138 passed and the runtime. Almost no hackathon project has a real test suite. This one image separates you from the field.
3A test that proves a safety claim Screenshot the source of test_tampered_envelope_fails_verification and test_payer_escalates_rather_than_finally_denying, side by side. Turns "we have human-in-the-loop" from a claim into something a judge can read in nine lines of code.
4The rule engine source backend/app/rules.py, the COVERAGE_RULES tuple and evaluate_request. Makes "every decision carries a rule ID" visibly true — the Decision return type cannot omit one.
5A live decision, with timing Terminal: the curl to /api/auth/request with -w '%{time_total}', showing the outcome, the rule ID and the elapsed time. The 88.6 ms claim, demonstrated rather than asserted.
6The audit trail for that request /audit in the UI filtered to that request, or GET /api/audit/{id} showing the five entries. Regulatory credibility. Five timestamped rows with before/after state is what a compliance reviewer looks for.

8The six-step demo /demo mid-run, plus a 15-second screen recording of the full ~11-second run. The recording is your insurance policy if live demo fails on the day. Bring it regardless.
9Clean production build cd frontend && npm run build — the route table with all 7 routes. Signals this is deployable software, not a prototype held together for a demo.
These are now section 3 of the Technical Execution Report — not a separate document The brief lists the Build Log as a section of the report, so artifacts 1, 2, 4 and 8 are embedded in section 3 of it: commit history, the green test run, a live decision, and the demo and dashboard screenshots. Re-capture them on your own machine first — a judge should see your terminal, not ours. The other five did not fit in eight pages; keep them on a laptop as Q&A backup, and appendix.html in the repo regenerates all nine.
Two things to get right before you capture anything Run cd zk && npm install first. Without snarkjs installed, 7 of the ZK tests fail on a missing dependency and your screenshot says "131 passed, 7 failed" — which reads as broken code rather than a missing package. With it installed you get a clean 138.
Update the README. It still says 129 tests; the suite is now 138. A judge who runs the command and gets a different number than your documentation claims will trust the rest of your numbers less. It is a one-line fix.

BSlide-by-slide, continued

06It works — liveNew. The most important slide in the deck.
Layout: Almost no text. A large embedded screen recording of the /demo run, or a live browser window. Beneath it, three numbers only: 88.6 ms median decision · 5 audit entries per request · 0 humans involved.

Run it live if the room's network allows, but have the recording embedded in the file as the fallback — not on a laptop, in the deck. The failure mode where you spend ninety seconds troubleshooting wifi is the one that costs you the round.

Say while it runs: "Nothing on this screen is mocked. The decision, the signatures, the proof and the audit trail are all produced by the code in our repository, right now."

07When the AI is wrongNew. Pre-empts the judges' first hard question.
Layout: The escalation ladder as six numbered rungs down the slide. Each rung: the failure on the left, the automated response on the right. Highlight rung 5 — appeal rejected → ESCALATED, never DENIED.

Lead with the line: "A machine cannot issue a final denial in our system. Not as policy — there is no code path that does it." Then show the test name that proves it. Every judge on a healthcare panel is waiting to ask what happens when the model is wrong; answering before they ask changes the tenor of the whole Q&A.

08CompetitionSame table, one new column
Layout: The Round 1 comparison table. Add a final column: "Working code today?" — Pavo: yes, with the repo link. Others: n/a, they are shipped products.
Be careful with this slide Cohere and Availity are real companies with real revenue. A column implying they have not shipped will read as naïve. Frame it as "we have built the thing that does not exist yet", not "we are ahead of them." Your honest position is: they own the market, you are betting on a capability none of them has, on a regulatory clock. That is a stronger and more credible story than claiming to be ahead.
11Unit economicsNew. Round 1 had none.
Layout: Two columns — provider group and payer. Rows: CAC, 3-year LTV, LTV:CAC, payback. Then one line across the bottom: marginal cost per authorization: $0.00057 against a $3.00 fee.

Volunteer the weak number. Say: "The payer segment is 2.3× LTV to CAC with a fifteen-month payback — it does not pay for itself as a product. We fund it anyway, because signing one payer makes us reachable by every provider group that submits to them. It is a distribution channel, not a profit centre." Judges remember the team that named its own weak spot before they found it.

12Financial projectionsSame totals, now derived
Layout: Three bars — $1.0M / $8.2M / $36.1M — with the driver beneath each (55 → 330 → 1,400 provider groups). Then a single sensitivity line: if we are wrong on all four key assumptions, 2028 is $13.8M, not $36.1M.

Gross margin trajectory as a small inset: 31% → 82% → 87%. Note aloud that Year 1 is 31%, not break-even as Round 1 implied, and that you corrected it yourselves after rebuilding the model bottom-up. Correcting your own number is a credibility gain, not a loss.

CSlide 9 — Current Traction

Read this first: the honest position The brief defines traction as "user surveys conducted, pilot tests run, or partnerships formed." The repository was searched for evidence of all three. There is none — and no reason there would be. It contains code, tests and a build specification: no survey responses, no pilot records, no letters of intent, no design-partner agreements.

DQuestions the judges will ask, and how to answer

Every one of these has an answer grounded in the code. Know which file each lives in.

QuestionAnswer
"What happens when your AI gets it wrong?" It escalates. There is no code path that approves or denies without a matching rule, and the rule set ends in a catch-all whose outcome is ESCALATED. A rejected appeal returns ESCALATED, never DENIED. Both are covered by tests — we can show you the assertions.
"Is this just a wrapper around a language model?" No — and this is the architectural point we are most confident about. No model touches an authorization decision. Inference is confined to document parsing, CPT mapping and appeal drafting, all thresholded. If our model provider went down, prior authorization would keep working.
"Why would a payer ever let you do this?" Because CMS-0057-F requires them to build FHIR prior-authorization APIs by January 2027 regardless. We are the first thing to use rails they are already obliged to lay. And it is cheaper for them: $3.00 against the $15–$40 they spend handling a request manually.
"How do you handle PHI?" Patient identifiers are SHA-256 hashed before the first database write — a test asserts the raw value never appears in storage. Beyond that, the zero-knowledge circuit proves three coverage criteria without transmitting age, diagnosis or deductible at all.
"Five rules is not a product." Agreed, and we say so in the report. Five rules prove the mechanism. The commercial build is clinical informaticists encoding each payer's published criteria — it is the largest line in our cost of revenue in all three years, and it is the moat. A competitor can copy the architecture in a quarter; they cannot copy two thousand encoded criteria.
"Your LTV:CAC looks too good." The blended figure is 8.6×, and it is high because authorization volume carries almost no marginal cost. The number to look at is the payer segment: 2.3× with a fifteen-month payback. We fund it because it is a distribution channel, not because it pays for itself.
"What is the biggest risk?" Provider group count, not price or regulation. Authorization fees are 73% of 2028 revenue and they scale with groups. Halving the group count costs us more than halving payers and cutting price combined — the sensitivity table shows it.
"What does not work yet?" Name them before they find them: the ZK diagnosis check reveals which covered diagnosis the patient has; the trusted setup is local and not production-safe; NPI verification is a flag rather than a live CMS lookup; facility pricing is mocked; the predictive classifier is not built, so non-definitive cases escalate. All are in Table 7 of the report.

The thing to remember on the day Round 1 asked you to argue that the human bottleneck in prior authorization can be removed. Round 2 asks you to prove you are building. You have the rarer half of that: working, tested, measured software. The gap is commercial validation — nobody outside your team has used this or committed to using it. Close as much of that gap as you can in the weeks you have, and be straightforward about whatever remains open. A team that has built the thing and is honest about not yet having customers reads far better than one claiming traction it cannot evidence.
